Heat Pump vs Heating Oil: What Actually Decides Which Costs Less

The answer comes down to two ratios: how expensive electricity is compared with oil, and how efficiently your heat pump turns each kilowatt-hour into heat. Everything else, including upfront cost, incentives and comfort, matters too, but running cost is determined by these.

The break-even electricity price

An oil boiler at 85% efficiency delivers 117,725 BTU per gallon. A heat pump with a seasonal COP of 2.5 delivers 8,530 BTU per kWh. Setting the two costs per MMBtu equal gives the electricity price at which they tie. Below it, the heat pump is cheaper to run; above it, oil is.

Why COP is the swing factor

Heat pump efficiency falls as outdoor temperatures drop, so a unit that reaches a COP of 3 in mild weather may average much lower over a cold winter. Use a seasonal figure for your climate, from the manufacturer's cold-climate data if available, rather than the best-case number. Moving from COP 2.0 to 3.0 raises the break-even electricity price by half, which is often the difference between a heat pump winning and losing.

What the running-cost math leaves out

  • Purchase and installation cost for the heat pump, duct or distribution work, and any electrical panel upgrade.
  • Incentives: federal, state and utility programs change often, so check current ones before deciding.
  • Backup heat for the coldest days, which may use oil or electric resistance and costs more per MMBtu.
  • Maintenance and fuel storage: oil needs tank and burner service; heat pumps need filter and coil care.
  • Future price changes for either fuel.
