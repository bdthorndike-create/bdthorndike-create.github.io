BTU, MMBtu, Therm and kWh: The Units Behind Heating Costs

Heating fuels are sold in different units: gallons, therms, kilowatt-hours, tons, cords. To compare them fairly, convert everything to one unit of delivered heat. This site uses the MMBtu (one million BTU).

The basic conversions

  • BTU: a small unit of heat energy.
  • MMBtu: 1,000,000 BTU. (The "MM" is the traditional Roman-numeral-style "thousand thousand".)
  • Therm: 100,000 BTU, so 10 therms = 1 MMBtu. Natural gas is often billed in therms or in hundreds or thousands of cubic feet (Ccf, Mcf); one Mcf is roughly 10 therms.
  • kWh: 3,412 BTU, so about 293 kWh = 1 MMBtu.

Efficiency is what turns fuel into heat

Furnaces and boilers are rated by AFUE, the share of the fuel's energy that ends up as heat in the house. An 85% oil boiler delivers 0.85 of each gallon's energy. Electric resistance heat is essentially 100% efficient at the point of use. A heat pump moves heat rather than making it, so it can deliver more than 100%: a seasonal coefficient of performance (COP) of 2.5 means 250%. Manufacturers often publish HSPF or HSPF2 instead; dividing HSPF by 3.412 gives a rough COP, though real results depend on your climate and installation.

The formula

Cost per MMBtu = price per unit ÷ (BTU per unit × efficiency ÷ 1,000,000)

Example: heating oil at $5.26 per gallon in an 85% boiler. A gallon holds 138,500 BTU, so it delivers 138,500 × 0.85 = 117,725 BTU. Cost per MMBtu = 5.26 ÷ 0.117725 ≈ $44.68. A heat pump at 18 cents per kWh and a COP of 2.5 delivers 3,412 × 2.5 = 8,530 BTU per kWh, so 0.18 ÷ 0.00853 ≈ $21.10 per MMBtu.
